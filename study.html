1. Values & principles of the UK

≈ 20 min · easy marks — most wrong answers here are obviously unkind or extreme

The UK expects everyone who lives here — not just citizens — to share a small set of values. Think of it as a deal: the UK gives you freedoms, and in return you take on responsibilities. The test asks about both lists, and about the principles that hold the whole system together.

The five fundamental principles

These are the ideas British public life is built on. Each one is simple once you see what it protects.

  • Democracy — people choose who governs them by voting.
  • The rule of law — everyone, including the government and the police, must obey the same laws.
  • Individual liberty — you are free to live your own life within the law.
  • Tolerance of those with different faiths and beliefs — you respect other people's right to believe differently.
  • Participation in community life — you play your part, for example by volunteering or voting.

What the UK offers you — the freedoms

  • Freedom of belief and religion — you can follow any religion, or none.
  • Freedom of speech — you can say what you think, within the law.
  • Freedom from unfair discrimination — you can't be treated worse because of who you are.
  • A right to a fair trial — if accused, you're heard by an independent court.
  • A right to join in the election of a government — you get a say in who runs the country.

What the UK expects of you — the responsibilities

  • Respect and obey the law.
  • Respect the rights of others, including their right to their own opinions.
  • Treat others with fairness.
  • Look after yourself and your family.
  • Look after the area in which you live and the environment.
Remember it: the responsibilities move outward in circles — the law → other people → yourself and family → your area. If an answer option sounds unkind, selfish or extreme ("intolerance", "autocracy", "pay as little tax as possible", "only mix with your own group"), it's wrong.

The test itself

You get 45 minutes to answer 24 questions, and you pass with 75% — that's 18 correct. It costs £50, you must book online, and a pass never expires. To apply for settlement or citizenship you also need to show you can speak and read English. At a citizenship ceremony, new citizens swear or affirm allegiance to the monarch and make a pledge to respect the UK's rights, freedoms and laws.

2. What is the UK?

≈ 20 min

The UK is a family of four nations on a group of islands in north-west Europe. The confusing part is the names: "Great Britain", "the UK" and "the British Isles" mean different things — and some islands are linked to the UK without being part of it. Get those straight and this whole section is easy marks.

The names, from smallest to largest

  • Great Britain is the big island: England, Scotland and Wales only.
  • The United Kingdom adds Northern Ireland. Its full name is "the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland".
  • The British Isles is the whole group of islands — including the Republic of Ireland, which is a separate, independent country.

Places linked to the UK but not part of it

  • Crown dependencies are the Channel Islands (Jersey, Guernsey) and the Isle of Man — they have their own governments.
  • British overseas territories are further away, such as St Helena and the Falkland Islands.
Remember it: Crown dependencies are close by (in the seas around Britain); overseas territories are overseas. Neither is part of the UK.

Each nation has its own capital, patron saint and flower. Learn them as a row in this table — questions often swap one detail for another.

The Union Flag

The flag — also called the Union Jack — is literally three flags laid on top of each other, one for each kingdom that joined the union. Its current form dates from 1801, when Ireland joined.

  • St George's cross (England) is a red cross on white.
  • St Andrew's cross (Scotland) is a white diagonal cross on blue.
  • St Patrick's cross (Ireland) is a red diagonal cross on white.
  • Wales is not on it, because Wales was already united with England by then. The Welsh flag has a red dragon instead.

Everyday facts

  • The currency is the pound sterling (£). Scotland and Northern Ireland print their own banknotes, and these are valid everywhere in the UK.
  • Coins are 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 and £2 — there is no 25p coin. Notes are £5, £10, £20 and £50, the highest.
  • Welsh is widely spoken in Wales and taught in schools; Gaelic is spoken in parts of Scotland; Irish Gaelic in parts of Northern Ireland.
  • The longest distance on the mainland is from John O'Groats in Scotland to Land's End in Cornwall — about 870 miles.
  • The population was just over 62 million in 2010, with about 84% living in England. Almost 10% of people have a parent or grandparent born outside the UK.

3. History: Stone Age to 1714

≈ 90 min · the biggest topic — many questions come from history

British history is a long chain of arrivals: hunters, then farmers, then Romans, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings and Normans. After 1066 nobody successfully invades England again, and the story turns inward — kings struggling with nobles, with Parliament and with the Church, until Parliament wins. Hold on to that shape and the dates hang off it.

Early Britain — from hunters to farmers

The first people were hunter-gatherers who walked here, because Britain was joined to the continent by a land bridge. It became an island about 10,000 years ago.

  • The first farmers arrived about 6,000 years ago, probably from south-east Europe. Settling down let them build monuments.
  • They built Stonehenge in Wiltshire, and the stone village of Skara Brae on Orkney — the best-preserved prehistoric village in northern Europe.
  • In the Bronze Age (about 4,000 years ago) people learned to work metal and buried their dead in round barrows.
  • In the Iron Age people built hill forts such as Maiden Castle in Dorset, spoke Celtic languages, and made the first coins minted in Britain — some carrying the names of Iron Age kings.

The Romans (AD 43–410)

Rome tried twice. Julius Caesar's invasion in 55 BC failed; Emperor Claudius succeeded in AD 43. The Romans then stayed about 400 years and left in AD 410 to defend the rest of their empire.

  • Boudicca, queen of the Iceni tribe in eastern England, led a revolt against them. Her statue stands on Westminster Bridge.
  • The Romans never conquered Scotland. Emperor Hadrian built a wall to keep out the Picts; you can still visit forts on it at Housesteads and Vindolanda.
  • They left behind roads, public buildings, a system of law, new plants and animals — and the first Christian communities (3rd–4th centuries).

Anglo-Saxons and Vikings

When the Romans left, tribes from northern Europe — the Jutes, Angles and Saxons — moved in. They weren't Christian at first, so missionaries came to convert them.

  • An Anglo-Saxon king was buried with treasure in a ship at Sutton Hoo in Suffolk.
  • St Patrick converted Ireland, St Columba founded a monastery on Iona, and St Augustine became the first Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • The Vikings came from Denmark and Norway, first raiding in AD 789.
  • Alfred the Great united the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and defeated them. Vikings still settled in the east and north, an area called the Danelaw, and a Danish king, Cnut, later ruled England.
  • In the north, the Viking threat pushed people to unite under one king, Kenneth MacAlpin — the start of "Scotland".

1066 — the Norman Conquest

William, Duke of Normandy (northern France) defeated and killed King Harold at the Battle of Hastings in 1066. It was the last successful foreign invasion of England — which is why everything after it is about power struggles inside the country.

  • The battle is shown on the Bayeux Tapestry, which is now displayed in France.
  • William recorded every town, landowner and animal in England in the Domesday Book, and built the Tower of London.
  • The rulers spoke Norman French while ordinary people spoke Anglo-Saxon. Over time the two blended into English.
Remember it: two records tell us about William I's England — the Domesday Book (a list) and the Bayeux Tapestry (a picture).

The Middle Ages — kings limited, nations shaped

This period has three themes: kings learning they're not above the law, England fighting its neighbours, and disaster changing society.

  • Magna Carta, 1215: nobles forced King John to accept that even the king must obey the law.
  • Parliament began to develop, with a House of Lords (nobles and bishops) and a House of Commons. Judges built up "common law" by following earlier decisions (precedent).
  • Wales was conquered by Edward I, made official by the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284); he built castles such as Conwy and Caernarvon.
  • Scotland stayed free: Robert the Bruce beat the English at Bannockburn in 1314.
  • In Ireland the English controlled only "the Pale", an area around Dublin.
  • English kings fought France in the Hundred Years War — which actually lasted 116 years. Henry V won at Agincourt in 1415. Knights also fought in the Crusades for the Holy Land.
  • The Black Death (probably plague) arrived in 1348 and killed about one third of England. With so few workers left, peasants could demand higher wages — society shifted.
  • Most peasants were serfs, who farmed a small plot of their lord's land and worked his land too.
  • Chaucer wrote The Canterbury Tales (pilgrims travelling to Canterbury), and William Caxton became the first person in England to print books.

The Wars of the Roses (1455–1485)

Two branches of the royal family fought for the crown: the House of Lancaster (red rose) and the House of York (white rose). It ended at the Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485, where Richard III (York) was killed. The Lancastrian winner, Henry Tudor, became Henry VII and married Elizabeth of York — so the red and white roses combined into the Tudor rose.

The Tudors (1485–1603) — religion splits the country

The Tudor century is about religion. Henry VIII breaks from the Pope for personal reasons, and his three children swing the country back and forth between Protestant and Catholic.

  • Henry VIII wanted to divorce Catherine of Aragon; when the Pope refused, he created the Church of England, with the king — not the Pope — in charge. This was part of the wider Reformation, a movement against the Pope's authority.
  • Henry also formally united Wales with England.
  • His six wives: Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn (executed at the Tower; mother of Elizabeth I), Jane Seymour (died after giving birth to Edward), Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr (outlived him).
  • His son Edward VI was strongly Protestant — the Book of Common Prayer was written in his reign. Then Mary I ("Bloody Mary") was Catholic and persecuted Protestants.
  • Elizabeth I (1558–1603) was Protestant but took a middle way between the extremes. Her navy defeated the Spanish Armada in 1588. Explorers like Sir Francis Drake sailed round the world, and settlers began colonising America's east coast.
  • Shakespeare (1564–1616), from Stratford-upon-Avon, wrote in this age: Romeo and Juliet, Hamlet, Macbeth, A Midsummer Night's Dream.
  • In Scotland, Parliament ended the Pope's authority in 1560 and set up a Protestant church — but, unlike England's, not a state church. The Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots fled to England and was executed for plotting against Elizabeth.
Remember it: Henry VIII's wives — divorced, beheaded, died · divorced, beheaded, survived. His children — Edward Protestant, Mary Catholic, Elizabeth middle way.

The Stuarts (1603–1714) — king versus Parliament

The big Stuart story is a fight over who really rules. Kings who claimed power from God clashed with Parliament, which led to civil war, a republic, a return of the monarchy, and finally the deal that still exists today: Parliament holds the real power.

  • 1603: Elizabeth died childless, so James VI of Scotland also became James I of England — one king, two separate countries. He ordered the King James Bible (the "Authorised Version"). Protestant settlers were given land in Ulster — the plantations.
  • Charles I believed in the "Divine Right of Kings" and tried to rule without Parliament. War broke out in 1642: Cavaliers (for the king) against Roundheads (for Parliament), with battles at Marston Moor and Naseby. Many Roundheads were Puritans.
  • Parliament won and Charles I was executed in 1649. England became a republic, the Commonwealth, led by Oliver Cromwell as Lord Protector until he died in 1658.
  • His son Charles II, defeated by Cromwell, had famously hidden in an oak tree before fleeing abroad. He returned as king in 1660 — the Restoration. His reign saw the Great Plague (1665), the Great Fire of London (1666) — after which Wren rebuilt St Paul's — and the Habeas Corpus Act (1679), which says no one can be held unlawfully.
  • In 1688, Protestants invited William of Orange to take the throne from Catholic James II — the "Glorious Revolution", glorious because there was no fighting in England. William beat James at the Battle of the Boyne (1690) in Ireland. In Scotland, the MacDonalds of Glencoe were massacred in 1692 for being late to swear loyalty to him.
  • The Bill of Rights (1689) limited the monarch's power and confirmed Parliament's — the start of constitutional monarchy. It did not give anyone new votes.
  • Refugees arrived too: Huguenots — French Protestants — between 1680 and 1720.
  • The Act of Union, 1707 joined England and Scotland as the Kingdom of Great Britain. Scotland kept its own law, education and church.
  • Science flourished: Isaac Newton showed that gravity applies across the whole universe.
Remember it: the king-vs-Parliament story in four numbers — 1642 war starts · 1649 king executed · 1660 king restored · 1688 Parliament chooses the king.

4. History: 1714 to today

≈ 75 min

With Parliament now in charge, Britain's energy goes into trade, industry and empire. Machines powered by steam transform the country, the empire grows to be the largest in history, and ordinary people gradually win rights — the vote, fair working hours, free healthcare. In the 20th century two world wars and the end of empire reshape the country into the modern UK.

The 18th century — ideas, industry and empire

  • Sir Robert Walpole became the first Prime Minister (1721–42), as power moved from the monarch to Parliament.
  • The last attempt to bring back the Stuarts came from Bonnie Prince Charlie, backed by Highland clans. He was defeated at Culloden in 1746. Later, in the Highland Clearances, landlords cleared small farms for sheep and cattle, and many Scots emigrated.
  • The Enlightenment brought new ideas: Adam Smith on economics, David Hume on human nature, and James Watt's work on steam power. Robert Burns wrote Auld Lang Syne, sung at New Year.
  • The Industrial Revolution moved Britain from farming to factories, all driven by steam power. Richard Arkwright ran efficient spinning mills; canals linked factories to towns and ports; the Bessemer process mass-produced steel; Brunel built the Great Western Railway.
  • France was Britain's main rival for trade and colonies. Captain Cook mapped the coast of Australia. The American colonies rebelled against British taxes and declared independence in 1776.
  • Sake Dean Mahomet opened Britain's first curry house (1810) and introduced "shampooing" (head massage).

Ending slavery

Britain's trade also included the slave trade, taking people mainly from West Africa to the Americas. A campaign led in Parliament by William Wilberforce ended it in stages:

  • 1807 — trading slaves in British ships and ports was banned.
  • 1833 — slavery itself was abolished throughout the British Empire. Around 2 million workers then came from India and China to replace the freed slaves.

The 19th century — the Victorian age

  • Admiral Nelson beat the French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar (1805) but was killed; his ship HMS Victory is in Portsmouth. The Duke of Wellington finally beat Napoleon at Waterloo (1815) — the last major battle between Britain and France.
  • In 1801 Ireland joined the union, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.
  • Queen Victoria reigned for almost 64 years, 1837–1901. Britain produced over half the world's iron, coal and cotton cloth, and promoted free trade — for example by repealing the Corn Laws (1846).
  • Prince Albert organised the Great Exhibition of 1851 in the Crystal Palace, Hyde Park. The population then was about 20 million.
  • The Irish famine of the 1840s, caused by the failure of the potato crop, killed a million people and drove 1.5 million to emigrate. Some Irish nationalists, the Fenians, wanted full independence.
  • In the Crimean War (1853–56) Britain fought Russia, alongside France and Turkey — the first war reported with news photographs. Florence Nightingale nursed soldiers there and became the founder of modern nursing.
  • Rights slowly grew: the Reform Act 1832 gave more men the vote; the Factories Act 1847 limited women and children to 10 hours a day; the Married Women's Property Acts (1870, 1882) let wives keep their own money and property.
  • About 120,000 Russian and Polish Jews came to Britain between 1870 and 1914 to escape persecution.
  • The Boer War (1899–1902) was fought in South Africa against settlers of Dutch origin. Rudyard Kipling wrote The Jungle Book.

Votes for women

Emmeline Pankhurst founded the Women's Franchise League (1889) and then led the suffragettes, who used protest and civil disobedience. Their campaign — and women's war work — paid off in two steps:

  • 1918 — women over 30 got the vote.
  • 1928 — women got the vote at 21, the same as men. (The voting age for everyone dropped to 18 in 1969.)
Remember it: 18 → 30, 28 → 21. In 1918 the age was 30; in 1928 it became 21.

The First World War (1914–1918)

The war was triggered by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria in 1914. The Battle of the Somme (from 1 July 1916) cost about 60,000 British casualties on the first day alone. The war ended at 11am on 11 November 1918 — which is why we remember it at 11am on 11 November each year.

Meanwhile Ireland had been promised Home Rule, but the war delayed it. After the Easter Rising (1916), Ireland split in 1922: the south became the Irish Free State (a republic in 1949) and Northern Ireland stayed in the UK. Later conflict there is called "the Troubles".

Between the wars and the Second World War (1939–1945)

The Great Depression of the 1930s hit traditional industries like shipbuilding hard. Then, when Germany invaded Poland in 1939, Britain and France declared war. Winston Churchill became PM in 1940 and kept the country fighting with famous speeches; in 2002 he was voted the greatest Briton ever.

  • Dunkirk (1940): about 300,000 soldiers were rescued from France, helped by many civilian small boats — the "Dunkirk spirit".
  • Battle of Britain (summer 1940): the RAF, flying Spitfires and Hurricanes, beat the German air force.
  • The Blitz: German bombing of London, Coventry and other cities.
  • D-Day, 6 June 1944: Allied forces landed in Normandy. The war ended in 1945, and the United Nations was set up afterwards.

After 1945 — building the modern UK

After the war, voters chose a Labour government under Clement Attlee (1945–51) to rebuild the country around one idea: the state should look after people "from cradle to grave".

  • The Beveridge Report (1942) laid the plan for the welfare state.
  • The NHS began in 1948, led by Health Minister Aneurin (Nye) Bevan.
  • The 1944 Education Act ("Butler Act") introduced free secondary education.
  • The empire became the Commonwealth: India and Pakistan became independent in 1947. To fill labour shortages, people came from the West Indies, India and Pakistan.
  • The 1960s ("Swinging Sixties") brought fashion, film and pop music — the Beatles and the Rolling Stones — and more liberal laws on divorce and abortion.
  • The UK joined the European Economic Community in 1973. (It left the EU on 31 January 2020.)
  • Margaret Thatcher, the first woman PM, served 1979–1990 — the longest of any 20th-century PM. In 1982 Argentina invaded the Falklands and British forces took them back. John Major followed and helped start the Northern Ireland peace process.
  • Tony Blair (1997–2007) brought devolution: the Scottish Parliament and Welsh Assembly opened in 1999, and the Good Friday Agreement (1998) brought peace and an Assembly to Northern Ireland.
  • In 2010 no party won a majority, so the Conservatives and Liberal Democrats formed a coalition. Queen Elizabeth II celebrated her Diamond Jubilee in 2012.

5. A modern, thriving society

≈ 75 min · lots of easy marks if you've read it once

This chapter is about everyday British life: what people believe, what they celebrate, the sport they watch, and the art, books and places the country is proud of. There's no story to follow here, so the trick is to group facts — each festival with its faith, each sportsperson with their sport, each writer with their book.

Religion

The UK is mainly Christian but increasingly varied. In the 2011 Census about 59% said they were Christian, about 5% Muslim, smaller numbers Hindu, Sikh, Jewish and Buddhist, and about 25% no religion.

  • England's state church is the Church of England (Anglican). The monarch is its Supreme Governor, and its spiritual leader is the Archbishop of Canterbury. Some of its bishops sit in the House of Lords.
  • Scotland's national church is the Church of Scotland, which is Presbyterian — run by ministers and elders and chaired by a Moderator.
  • Wales and Northern Ireland have no established church.

Sport

Many of the world's big sports were invented or shaped in Britain, so expect questions on where they come from and who the heroes are.

  • Golf goes back to 15th-century Scotland; St Andrews is "the home of golf". Rugby began in England in the early 1800s; the Six Nations is England, Scotland, Wales, Ireland, France and Italy.
  • Cricket's famous series against Australia is the Ashes. Football is the most popular sport; England won the 1966 World Cup under captain Bobby Moore.
  • Tennis: Wimbledon is the oldest tournament in the world. Rowing: Oxford and Cambridge race each year on the Thames (the Boat Race).
  • Horse racing: the Grand National at Aintree (near Liverpool), Royal Ascot (Berkshire), the Scottish Grand National at Ayr, and the National Horseracing Museum at Newmarket.
  • London hosted the Olympics in 1908, 1948 and 2012. The Paralympics grew from Dr Sir Ludwig Guttmann's work at Stoke Mandeville hospital.

Music & theatre

  • Handel (German-born) wrote the Water Music for George I; Holst wrote The Planets; Elgar wrote the Pomp and Circumstance marches; Britten wrote the opera Peter Grimes.
  • The Proms is an eight-week summer season of classical concerts, mainly at the Royal Albert Hall.
  • Big festivals include Glastonbury and Wales's National Eisteddfod (music, poetry and dance). Awards: the Mercury Prize (best album) and the Brit Awards.
  • London's West End is known as "Theatreland"; its awards are the Laurence Olivier Awards. The Edinburgh Festival and Fringe (mainly theatre and comedy) take place every summer.
  • Gilbert and Sullivan wrote comic operas; Andrew Lloyd Webber writes musicals. Pantomimes — comic plays based on fairy stories — are a Christmas tradition.

Art, architecture & design

  • Painters: Gainsborough (portraits), Constable (landscapes of Dedham Vale), Turner (landscapes), Lowry (northern industrial towns), David Hockney (1960s pop art). Sculptor: Henry Moore.
  • The Turner Prize (since 1984) is for contemporary art. Tate Britain and Tate Modern are in London.
  • Architects: Wren (St Paul's), Lutyens (the Cenotaph), and garden designers Capability Brown and Gertrude Jekyll. The Chelsea Flower Show is held every year.
  • Design: Chippendale (furniture), Clarice Cliff (Art Deco ceramics), Mary Quant and Vivienne Westwood (fashion).

Books, poems & film

  • Novels: Jane Austen — Pride and Prejudice; Dickens — Oliver Twist; Robert Louis Stevenson — Treasure Island; Thomas Hardy — Far from the Madding Crowd; Conan Doyle — Sherlock Holmes; Tolkien — The Lord of the Rings, voted the best-loved novel in 2003; J K Rowling — Harry Potter.
  • Nobel Prize winners: William Golding (Lord of the Flies), Seamus Heaney, Harold Pinter. The Man Booker Prize is for the best novel.
  • Poems: Beowulf (Anglo-Saxon hero fighting monsters), Chaucer, Milton (Paradise Lost), Wordsworth (daffodils, inspired by nature), Burns, Blake, Keats, Shelley, Byron, Tennyson. First World War poets: Wilfred Owen, Siegfried Sassoon. Welsh poet: Dylan Thomas.
  • Film: Charlie Chaplin (the tramp, silent films), Alfred Hitchcock (The 39 Steps, 1935), Nick Park (Wallace and Gromit, several Oscars). The James Bond and Harry Potter series are among the most successful ever. Film awards: the BAFTAs.
  • Comedy and satire: Punch magazine (1840s) and Monty Python's Flying Circus.

6. Government & democracy

≈ 50 min · frequently tested

Remember the Stuart story? It ended with Parliament on top — and that's still how it works. The monarch is head of state but doesn't decide policy. Voters elect MPs; the party with the most MPs forms the government; its leader becomes Prime Minister; and the second-largest party becomes the opposition, whose job is to challenge them. Some powers have since been handed down ("devolved") to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The constitution and the monarch

  • The UK is a parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarch. Its constitution is not written in one single document — it's built from laws, conventions and documents like Magna Carta.
  • Its constitutional institutions are the monarchy, Parliament, the PM and Cabinet, the judiciary, the police, the civil service and local government. (The armed forces are not one.)
  • The monarch is head of state — handbook: Queen Elizabeth II, heir Prince Charles (today: King Charles III since 2022; heir Prince William). The monarch opens each session of Parliament with a speech setting out the government's plans, meets the PM weekly to advise, warn and encourage, but cannot refuse laws Parliament has passed.
  • The national anthem is God Save the Queen / King.

Parliament — two Houses

  • The House of Commons has 650 MPs, each elected to represent everyone in a constituency. It's the more important House.
  • The Speaker is an MP chosen by other MPs to chair debates and keep order. The Speaker stays neutral but still represents their own constituents.
  • The House of Lords is not elected. Its members, called peers, are life peers (appointed by the monarch on the PM's advice), some hereditary peers, and senior Church of England bishops. The Lords checks and suggests changes to laws, but the Commons can overrule it. Because its members don't depend on votes, it's usually more independent of the government.
  • Whips make sure MPs turn up and vote with their party. What's said in Parliament is recorded in Hansard, and anyone can watch from the public galleries of both Houses.

Elections

  • A general election must be held at least every five years. MPs are elected by "first past the post" — whoever gets the most votes in a constituency wins.
  • If an MP dies or resigns, a by-election fills the seat. If no party wins a majority, two can join in a coalition, as in 2010.
  • Before an election, parties go canvassing (door-to-door or with leaflets), and radio and TV must give balanced coverage to all main parties. You get a poll card telling you where and when to vote.
  • You must be 18 to vote in a general election (since 1969) and must be on the electoral register, which you join through your local council — you can register at any time. Voting is secret. The handbook notes that Northern Ireland used individual registration (everyone fills in their own form) (the rest of the UK does too since 2014).
  • UK, Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens living here can vote in all elections. Most of them aged 18+ can also stand as an MP — but not civil servants, members of the armed forces, or people convicted of certain crimes.
  • You can contact your MP by letter or phone at their constituency office or Commons office, or meet them at a local surgery.

The government

  • The Prime Minister leads the largest party. The official home is 10 Downing Street; the country house is Chequers.
  • The PM chooses about 20 senior ministers for the Cabinet, including the Chancellor of the Exchequer (the economy), the Home Secretary (crime, policing, immigration) and the Foreign Secretary.
  • The Leader of the Opposition picks a Shadow Cabinet to challenge the government and offer alternatives, and questions the PM every week at Prime Minister's Questions.
  • Civil servants carry out the government's work. They're appointed on merit and must stay politically neutral.
  • Local councils are democratically elected and paid for partly by council tax. London has an elected Mayor.
  • The BBC is paid for by the TV licence fee but is independent of government. Pressure groups try to influence policy.

Devolution — power shared with the nations

Since 1997–99, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own law-making bodies for things like education and health. The UK government keeps the big national matters: defence, foreign affairs and immigration.

The UK and the world

  • The Commonwealth is a voluntary association of countries, mostly from the former empire, with the monarch as its head. It has no power over its members but can suspend one.
  • The UK is one of the five permanent members of the UN Security Council, and a member of NATO.
  • The Council of Europe is separate from the EU; it created the European Convention on Human Rights but cannot make laws.

7. The law & your role in the community

≈ 50 min

The law has two halves. Criminal law is the state punishing wrongdoing, through the police and courts. Civil law is about sorting out disputes between people, such as with a landlord or an employer. Around that sit your rights, your taxes, and the everyday rules (ages, driving, licences) that the test likes to check.

Criminal or civil?

  • Criminal: carrying a weapon, selling drugs, racial crime, selling alcohol or tobacco to under-18s, smoking in enclosed public places, driving without insurance.
  • Civil: disputes over housing, employment (including workplace discrimination), consumer rights and debt.

The courts

Minor cases go to local courts run by volunteers; serious ones go to higher courts with a jury. Scotland uses different names.

  • Magistrates (Justices of the Peace) are usually unpaid volunteers without legal qualifications.
  • A Youth Court is run by up to three trained magistrates or a District Judge. The public can't attend, and the media cannot publish the names or photos of the young people accused.
  • Jurors are picked at random from the electoral register, aged 18 to 70 (now up to 75 in England and Wales).
  • The small claims procedure settles minor disputes informally, without an expensive lawyer. Solicitors usually charge by the time they spend; Citizens Advice gives free advice.
  • Judges are independent of government; the highest court is the Supreme Court.

The police

  • The police protect life and property, keep the peace, and prevent and detect crime. PCSOs (Police Community Support Officers) support them on the streets. Since 2012, each area elects a Police and Crime Commissioner.
  • To complain about the police, go to a police station, write to the Chief Constable, or contact an independent complaints body.
  • Call 999 or 112 in an emergency and 101 otherwise. If anyone tries to recruit you into extremism, tell your local police.

Your rights and protection

  • The Human Rights Act 1998 brought the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law: the right to life, a fair trial, freedom of religion and expression, and freedom from torture and slavery.
  • The Equality Act 2010 bans discrimination on grounds such as age, disability, sex, race, religion and sexuality.
  • Domestic violence is a crime, whether or not the couple is married — and a husband who forces his wife to have sex can be charged with rape.
  • Forced marriage is illegal, and courts can issue Forced Marriage Protection Orders. FGM is illegal, including taking a girl abroad for it.

Money, work and tax

  • Income tax pays for national services such as roads, education, police and the armed forces. National Insurance pays for the state pension and the NHS. Council tax pays for local services. HMRC collects tax.
  • To work you need a National Insurance number, which you apply for through the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) with proof of identity and right to work.
  • Women make up about half the workforce. Most shops open seven days a week, with shorter hours on Sundays.

Being a good citizen

The values from Section 1 come back here in practical form: take part, help others, look after your area.

  • Volunteer — for example as a school governor, who sets a school's direction and checks how well it's doing, or with Neighbourhood Watch.
  • Give — blood, and organs through the NHS Organ Donor Register (living people can even donate a kidney).
  • Support charities — e.g. Friends of the Earth (environment), Age UK (older people), the NSPCC (children), Shelter (homeless people).
  • Look after the environment — recycle (it saves energy and reduces landfill), don't drop litter, shop locally.
