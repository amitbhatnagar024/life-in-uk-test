1. Values & principles of the UK

≈ 15 min · frequently tested

The fundamental principles of British life

  • Democracy
  • The rule of law — everyone, including government, must obey the law
  • Individual liberty
  • Tolerance of those with different faiths and beliefs
  • Participation in community life

Responsibilities if you want to live here permanently

  • Respect and obey the law
  • Respect the rights of others, including their right to their own opinions
  • Treat others with fairness
  • Look after yourself and your family
  • Look after the area in which you live and the environment

Freedoms the UK offers

Freedom of belief and religion · freedom of speech · freedom from unfair discrimination · a right to a fair trial · a right to join in the election of a government.

The test itself

24 questions 45 minutes pass mark 75% = 18 correct fee £50 a pass never expires
Trap answers: the wrong options are usually the opposite of a value — "intolerance", "communism", "pay as little tax as possible", "only mix with people of your own background". If an option sounds unkind or extreme, it's wrong.

2. What is the UK?

≈ 15 min
  • United Kingdom = England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • Great Britain = England, Scotland and Wales only.
  • British Isles = the whole group of islands, including the Republic of Ireland (which is a separate country).
  • Crown dependencies — the Channel Islands (Jersey, Guernsey) and the Isle of Man. Linked to the UK but not part of it; they have their own governments.
  • British overseas territories — e.g. the Falkland Islands, St Helena. Linked to the UK, not part of it.

The four nations

Memory hook: the saints' days run through the year in order D-P-G-A: David (Mar 1) → Patrick (Mar 17) → George (Apr 23) → Andrew (Nov 30). Only Scotland and Northern Ireland make theirs official holidays.

Key facts

  • Currency: pound sterling (£). Scotland and Northern Ireland print their own banknotes, valid everywhere in the UK.
  • Languages: English, plus Welsh (Wales), Gaelic (Scotland), Irish Gaelic (Northern Ireland).
  • Longest distance on the mainland: John O'Groats (north Scotland) to Land's End (Cornwall) — about 870 miles.
  • Population (handbook): just over 62 million in 2010. About 84% live in England, 8% Scotland, 5% Wales, under 3% Northern Ireland.

The Union Flag

Combines the crosses of St George (red cross on white, England), St Andrew (white diagonal on blue, Scotland) and St Patrick (red diagonal on white, Ireland). Created 1801. Wales is not represented — the Welsh flag has a red dragon.

3. History: Stone Age to 1714

≈ 75 min · the biggest topic — most questions come from history

Early Britain

  • Stone Age: hunter-gatherers. Britain was joined to the continent by a land bridge and became an island about 10,000 years ago.
  • First farmers arrived about 6,000 years ago, probably from south-east Europe.
  • Stonehenge (Wiltshire) — built by early farmers. Skara Brae (Orkney) — the best-preserved prehistoric village in northern Europe.
  • Bronze Age (~4,000 years ago): metalworking; dead buried in round barrows.
  • Iron Age: hill forts (e.g. Maiden Castle, Dorset). People spoke Celtic languages. Made the first coins minted in Britain, some inscribed with the names of Iron Age kings.

Romans (AD 43 – 410)

  • Julius Caesar invaded in 55 BC — unsuccessful.
  • Emperor Claudius invaded in AD 43 — successful.
  • Boudicca, queen of the Iceni (eastern England), fought the Romans. Her statue is on Westminster Bridge, London.
  • Hadrian's Wall was built to keep out the Picts (ancestors of the Scots).
  • Romans stayed about 400 years, leaving in AD 410. They brought roads, public buildings, law, new plants and animals. First Christian communities appeared in the 3rd and 4th centuries.

Anglo-Saxons & Vikings

  • After the Romans: Jutes, Angles and Saxons invaded. By about AD 600 there were Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.
  • Sutton Hoo (Suffolk) — burial site of an Anglo-Saxon king, buried with treasure in a ship.
  • Christian missionaries: St Patrick (Ireland), St Columba (monastery on Iona), St Augustine — first Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • Vikings from Denmark and Norway first came in AD 789.
  • King Alfred the Great united the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and defeated the Vikings. Vikings settled in the east and north (the Danelaw).
  • Cnut (Canute) — a Danish king who ruled England. In Scotland, Kenneth MacAlpin united the peoples under one king.

The Norman Conquest — 1066

  • William, Duke of Normandy defeated King Harold at the Battle of Hastings, 1066. Harold was killed.
  • The Bayeux Tapestry shows the battle. It was the last successful foreign invasion of England.
  • William ordered the Domesday Book — a list of all towns, villages, people, landowners and animals in England.

Middle Ages

  • Magna Carta, 1215 — King John was forced to agree to it. It established that even the king is subject to the law.
  • Wales conquered by Edward I; the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed Wales. Castles like Conwy and Caernarvon.
  • Battle of Bannockburn, 1314 — Robert the Bruce defeated the English; Scotland stayed unconquered.
  • In Ireland the English controlled only the Pale, an area around Dublin.
  • Crusades — knights fought for control of the Holy Land.
  • Hundred Years War with France — actually lasted 116 years (1337–1453). Battle of Agincourt, 1415 — won by Henry V.
  • Black Death, 1348 — plague killed about one third of England's population. Labour shortages followed, helping peasants.
  • Parliament developed: House of Lords (nobility, bishops) and House of Commons (knights, wealthy townspeople). Judges developed "common law" based on precedent.
  • Language: Norman French + Anglo-Saxon blended into English. Geoffrey Chaucer wrote The Canterbury Tales. William Caxton was the first person in England to print books.
  • Wars of the Roses (1455): House of Lancaster (red rose) vs House of York (white rose). Ended at the Battle of Bosworth Field, 1485 — Richard III killed; Henry Tudor became Henry VII, married Elizabeth of York and combined the roses into the red-and-white Tudor rose.

Tudors (1485 – 1603)

  • Henry VIII broke from Rome and established the Church of England because the Pope refused his divorce. He also formally united Wales with England.
  • His six wives, in order: Catherine of Aragon (divorced), Anne Boleyn (executed; mother of Elizabeth I), Jane Seymour (died after giving birth to Edward), Anne of Cleves (divorced), Catherine Howard (executed), Catherine Parr (survived him).
    Rhyme: divorced, beheaded, died · divorced, beheaded, survived.
  • Edward VI — Protestant; Book of Common Prayer. Mary I ("Bloody Mary") — Catholic, persecuted Protestants.
  • Elizabeth I (1558–1603) — Protestant; defeated the Spanish Armada in 1588. Never married, no children.
  • Sir Francis Drake — one of the first to sail around the world, in the Golden Hind.
  • William Shakespeare (1564–1616), born in Stratford-upon-Avon. Plays include Romeo and Juliet, Hamlet, Macbeth, A Midsummer Night's Dream.
  • Scotland: Mary, Queen of Scots — Catholic, fled to England, executed by Elizabeth I. Her son became James VI of Scotland.
  • Reformation in Scotland — the Church of Scotland (Presbyterian) was created.

Stuarts (1603 – 1714)

  • 1603: James VI of Scotland also became James I of England — one king, but still separate countries. He ordered the King James Bible.
  • Ireland: Protestant settlers given land in Ulster (the plantations).
  • Civil War, 1642: Cavaliers (king's supporters) vs Roundheads (Parliament). Parliament won; Charles I executed in 1649.
  • England became a republic, the Commonwealth. Oliver Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector until his death in 1658.
  • Restoration, 1660: Charles II became king. Great Plague (1665) and Great Fire of London (1666) — Sir Christopher Wren rebuilt St Paul's Cathedral. The Royal Society was founded.
  • Habeas Corpus Act, 1679 — no one can be held unlawfully; you must be brought before a court.
  • Glorious Revolution, 1688: Protestant William of Orange invaded; Catholic James II fled. William and Mary became joint rulers. Battle of the Boyne, 1690 (Ireland) — William defeated James.
  • Bill of Rights, 1689 — limited the monarch's power; a Catholic cannot be monarch.
  • Act of Union, 1707: England and Scotland united as the Kingdom of Great Britain. Scotland kept its own legal, education and church systems.
  • Science: Isaac Newton — gravity, light and colour.

4. History: 1714 to today

≈ 60 min

18th century

  • Sir Robert Walpole — the first Prime Minister (1721–1742).
  • Jacobites: Bonnie Prince Charlie (Charles Edward Stuart) tried to retake the throne; defeated at the Battle of Culloden, 1746. Later the Highland Clearances drove many Scots from their land.
  • The Enlightenment: Adam Smith (economics), David Hume (philosophy), James Watt (steam power). Robert Burns — Scotland's poet (Auld Lang Syne).
  • Industrial Revolution: Richard Arkwright (horse-driven spinning mill, efficient factories), Bessemer process (mass-produced steel), canals, railways. Isambard Kingdom Brunel — engineer of the Great Western Railway, tunnels, bridges and ships.
  • American War of Independence — 13 colonies declared independence in 1776.
  • Slave trade: William Wilberforce led the campaign. Trading slaves banned 1807; slavery abolished in the British Empire 1833. Afterwards 2 million migrants from India and China came to work.

19th century

  • Battle of Trafalgar, 1805 — Admiral Nelson defeated the French and Spanish fleets; he died in the battle. Nelson's Column in Trafalgar Square.
  • Battle of Waterloo, 1815 — the Duke of Wellington defeated Napoleon.
  • 1801: Act of Union with Ireland → the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland; the Union Flag created.
  • Queen Victoria (1837–1901) — the British Empire became the largest the world had seen.
  • Great Exhibition, 1851, in the Crystal Palace, Hyde Park — organised by Prince Albert.
  • Irish famine (mid-1840s) — potato crop failed; a million died, 1.5 million emigrated.
  • Reform Act 1832 — more men could vote; later reforms extended this further.
  • Women: Married Women's Property Acts (1870, 1882) let married women keep their own money and property. Florence Nightingale — nursing in the Crimean War; founder of modern nursing, set up a nursing school at St Thomas' Hospital, London.
  • Boer War (1899–1902) in South Africa. Rudyard Kipling — writer (The Jungle Book, poem If).

Women's vote

  • Emmeline Pankhurst led the suffragettes.
  • 1918: women over 30 could vote. 1928: women got the vote at 21, the same as men.

20th century

  • First World War, 1914–1918. Armistice at 11am on 11 November 1918. Battle of the Somme (1 July 1916) — about 60,000 British casualties on the first day.
  • Ireland: Home Rule promised in 1913 but delayed by the war. Easter Rising, 1916. Ireland split in 1921/22: the south became the Irish Free State (a republic in 1949); Northern Ireland stayed in the UK. Later conflict known as "the Troubles".
  • Great Depression (1930s) — mass unemployment; but new industries (cars, aviation) grew.
  • Second World War, 1939–1945: began when Hitler invaded Poland. Winston Churchill PM from 1940 (famous speeches: "We shall fight on the beaches"); voted the greatest Briton of all time in 2002.
    • Dunkirk, 1940 — 300,000 soldiers evacuated from France by small boats.
    • Battle of Britain, summer 1940 — RAF (Spitfires, Hurricanes) won the air battle against Germany.
    • The Blitz — German bombing of British cities (London, Coventry and others).
    • D-Day, 6 June 1944 — Allied landings in Normandy. Germany surrendered May 1945; Japan in August 1945 after atomic bombs.
  • Post-war Labour government: Clement Attlee PM (1945–51). NHS founded 1948 by Health Minister Aneurin (Nye) Bevan. Beveridge Report (1942) → the welfare state. R A Butler's 1944 Education Act — free secondary education.
  • Empire to Commonwealth: India and Pakistan independent in 1947. 1950s labour shortages → migrants from the West Indies, India and Pakistan.
  • 1960s — "Swinging Sixties": fashion, music, the Beatles; laws on divorce and abortion liberalised.
  • 1973: UK joined the European Economic Community (EEC). (The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 — not in the handbook.)
  • Margaret Thatcher — first woman PM, 1979–1990, the longest-serving PM of the 20th century.
  • Tony Blair (Labour) 1997–2007: devolution — Scottish Parliament and Welsh Assembly from 1999; Good Friday Agreement, 1998 for Northern Ireland. Then Gordon Brown; in 2010 a Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition (David Cameron PM, Nick Clegg deputy).

5. A modern, thriving society

≈ 60 min · lots of easy marks if you've skimmed it

Religion

  • About 59% identify as Christian; 4.8% Muslim, 1.5% Hindu, 0.8% Sikh, 0.5% Jewish, 0.4% Buddhist; about 25% no religion.
  • Church of England (Anglican) — the state church in England. The monarch is its Supreme Governor; the spiritual leader is the Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • Church of Scotland — Presbyterian, run by ministers and elders, chaired by the Moderator.
  • There is no state church in Wales or Northern Ireland.

Arts & culture

  • Music: Purcell; Handel (Water Music); Holst (The Planets); Elgar (Pomp and Circumstance); Britten (opera Peter Grimes). The Proms — 8 weeks of concerts, mainly at the Royal Albert Hall. Festivals: Glastonbury, Isle of Wight, the Welsh National Eisteddfod. Awards: Mercury Prize, Brit Awards.
  • Theatre: London's West End; Edinburgh Festival and Fringe; Laurence Olivier Awards. Gilbert & Sullivan comic operas; Andrew Lloyd Webber musicals. Pantomime at Christmas.
  • Art: Gainsborough (portraits), Constable (landscapes, Dedham Vale), Turner (landscapes), L S Lowry (northern industrial towns), Henry Moore (sculptor), David Hockney (1960s pop art). Turner Prize (since 1984) for contemporary art.
  • Architecture & design: Christopher Wren (St Paul's), Robert Adam, Capability Brown & Gertrude Jekyll (gardens), Edwin Lutyens (the Cenotaph). Chippendale (furniture), Clarice Cliff (ceramics), Mary Quant & Vivienne Westwood (fashion). Chelsea Flower Show.
  • Writers: Jane Austen (Pride and Prejudice), Charles Dickens (Oliver Twist), Robert Louis Stevenson (Treasure Island), Thomas Hardy, Arthur Conan Doyle (Sherlock Holmes), Evelyn Waugh, Graham Greene, J K Rowling (Harry Potter). Nobel Prize winners: William Golding, Seamus Heaney, Harold Pinter. Man Booker Prize for fiction.

  • Film & comedy: Charlie Chaplin, Alfred Hitchcock; Ealing comedies; James Bond; BAFTA awards. Punch magazine; Monty Python.

6. Government & democracy

≈ 45 min · frequently tested

The system

  • The UK is a parliamentary democracy with the monarch as head of state — a constitutional monarchy.
  • The constitution is not written in a single document — it is made up of laws, conventions and documents like Magna Carta, Habeas Corpus and the Bill of Rights.
  • The monarch: handbook — Queen Elizabeth II, heir Prince Charles (today: King Charles III since 2022, heir Prince William). The monarch cannot refuse laws passed by Parliament, opens each new session of Parliament (the State Opening and monarch's speech), and meets the PM weekly. National anthem: God Save the Queen/King.

Parliament

  • House of Commons — 650 MPs, each elected for a constituency. The most important chamber.
  • The Speaker — an MP elected by other MPs; politically neutral; chairs debates and keeps order.
  • House of Lords — not elected. Life peers (appointed by the monarch on the PM's advice), some hereditary peers, and senior Church of England bishops. It checks laws and can suggest changes, but the Commons can overrule it.
  • General elections at least every 5 years. Voting system: first past the post — the candidate with the most votes wins. If an MP dies or resigns: a by-election.
  • Whips — make sure MPs attend and vote with their party.

Government

  • Prime Minister — usually the leader of the party with the most MPs. Lives at 10 Downing Street; country house is Chequers.
  • The Cabinet — about 20 senior ministers, e.g. the Chancellor of the Exchequer (economy), the Home Secretary (crime, policing, immigration), the Foreign Secretary.
  • The Opposition — the second-largest party. Its leader leads a Shadow Cabinet and questions the PM at Prime Minister's Questions each week.
  • Civil servants carry out government policy and are politically neutral.
  • Local government — councils, funded partly by council tax. Some towns have elected mayors (e.g. London).

  • Devolved: education, health, agriculture, environment, local transport and similar.
  • Not devolved (UK Parliament keeps them): defence, foreign affairs, immigration, most taxation and social security.
  • The Scottish Parliament can pass laws on all matters not reserved to Westminster; the NI Assembly has at times been suspended.

Voting

  • Voting age: 18 for UK general elections (16 in Scottish and Welsh parliament and local elections).
  • Who can vote in all elections: UK citizens, and Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK.
  • You must be on the electoral register; councils update it every year. Ballot is secret; you can vote in person at a polling station or by post.
  • Most citizens aged 18+ can stand as an MP. You can contact your MP by letter, phone, or at their local "surgery".

UK and the world

  • Commonwealth — association of countries, mostly former British Empire. Handbook: 54 members (now 56). The monarch is its head. Membership is voluntary.
  • United Nations — the UK is one of the five permanent members of the Security Council. Also in NATO.
  • Council of Europe — separate from the EU; created the European Convention on Human Rights. It has no power to make laws.
  • European Union — UK joined the EEC in 1973. (UK left the EU on 31 January 2020.)

7. The law & your role in the community

≈ 40 min

Criminal vs civil law

  • Criminal law — crimes investigated by police and punished by courts: carrying weapons, selling drugs, racial crime, selling alcohol to under-18s, smoking in public places.
  • Civil law — disputes between people: housing, employment, consumer rights, debt, discrimination.

The courts

  • Magistrates (Justices of the Peace) are usually unpaid volunteers with no legal qualifications.
  • Small claims procedure — an informal way to settle minor disputes without an expensive lawyer.
  • Judges are independent of government. The highest court is the Supreme Court.
  • Help: solicitors and Citizens Advice.

The police

  • Duties: protect life and property, prevent disturbances (keep the peace), prevent and detect crime.
  • Police Community Support Officers (PCSOs) support police on the streets.
  • Police and Crime Commissioners (PCCs) — elected since November 2012.
  • Complaints go to an independent body (handbook: the IPCC in England and Wales; Police Ombudsman in Northern Ireland).
  • In an emergency call 999 or 112; non-emergency 101.

Rights & equality

  • Human Rights Act 1998 brought the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law: right to life, freedom from torture and slavery, right to a fair trial, freedom of thought, conscience, religion and expression.
  • Equality Act 2010 — illegal to discriminate on grounds of age, disability, sex, pregnancy, race, religion, sexuality, marital status and others.
  • Illegal: domestic violence (including between spouses), forced marriage, female genital mutilation (FGM).

Taxes & driving

  • Income tax, National Insurance (pays for state pension and NHS — get a National Insurance number), council tax for local services. Collected by HMRC.
  • Driving: at least 17 for a car licence. Must have insurance. Cars over 3 years old need an annual MOT test.

Being a good citizen

  • Jury service — anyone on the electoral register aged 18 to 70 can be asked to serve (in England & Wales now up to 75).
  • Volunteering — charities, school governors, neighbourhood watch, helping elderly neighbours.
  • Donate blood and organs — sign the NHS Organ Donor Register.
  • Look after the environment — recycle, don't drop litter, shop locally, walk or use public transport.

Exam technique

  • About 1 minute per question is plenty — 45 minutes for 24 questions.
  • Some questions ask you to pick two answers — read the question carefully.
  • "True or false" questions: an absolute word ("always", "never", "only") is often a clue it's false.
  • Bring the photo ID you booked with. You'll get the result the same day.
